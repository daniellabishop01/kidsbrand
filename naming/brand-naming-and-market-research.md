# Kid's Focus Relaunch: Brand Naming & Market Research

*Prepared Oct 9, 2026 as input to the "Pick a brand name" decision in the Team Overview (Phases 1–4).*

---

## 1. Bottom line

1. **Scouts is a good idea, but it has two real problems.** It sounds like explorers who notice things, which is positive and fits kids. But the word "Scouts" in a kids' brand belongs, in parents' minds, to Scouting America and Girl Scouts of the USA. Those two groups went to federal court with each other over "Scout" (2018–2022). The name also feels young to 13–17 year olds, who tend to leave scouting behind at that age.
2. **The structure matters more than the word.** Hiya and OLLY both use **one friendly master brand plus plain benefit names for each product** (e.g., *Hiya Kids Bedtime Essentials*, *OLLY Focus Buddies*). Do the same here, so the brand name never needs the word "kids". The teen line then becomes *[Brand] Teen Focus* without a rebrand.
3. **The brand will sell more than brain supplements, so the master brand can't be about focus.** Focus & Calm is the hero product. The master brand has to work just as well on sleep, immunity, gut, hydration and greens.
4. **Recommendation:** use **Wayfinder** as the master brand (it keeps the Scouts explorer idea without the Scouting organizations' word) and **Wits** as the hero product's name: *Wayfinder Wits, Focus & Calm*. Test **Wayfinder vs. Scout vs. Field Day** with parents using cheap Meta ads, following Molly Pittman's method, while a lawyer runs the trademark checks (sections 6–7).
5. **There is a competitor the outline doesn't cover:** OLLY already sells kids' focus and calm products: **Focus Buddies** (Cognizin citicoline, ages 4+) and **Mellow Buddies** (L-theanine), at Target since 2024. Add both to the Phase 2 competitor review. OLLY sells focus and calm as two separate products, while ours covers both in one study. That difference is a selling point.

## 3. Competitor analysis: Hiya

**Snapshot:** Founded in March 2020 by two LA dads, Darren Litt and Adam Gillman. Kids' chewable vitamins for ages 2–12, sold direct to consumers by subscription. About $103M in sales for the 12 months to Sep 30, 2024, with more than $134M projected for 2025. USANA bought 78.8% in Dec 2024 (reported at $260M; sources differ). Now also sold at Target.

**What drives their growth**
1. **Subscriptions are the engine.** The first box comes with a reusable glass bottle and stickers, and refills arrive in pouches after that. USANA said the subscription model was what it was buying.
2. **They sell an experience kids love, not just a pill.** In Litt's words: "We see our product not just as the vitamin you put in your mouth, but an experience that kids love." Kids decorate the bottle and get a game that changes every month.
3. **Licensed characters** (Disney Princess, Moana, Lion King, Barbie, Hot Wheels) only change the art. The formula stays the same. "Kids don't wake up excited about vitamins; they wake up excited about characters."
4. **They pick a clear enemy:** sugary gummies, and melatonin in sleep products. Every ad says "no added sugar, no gummy additives".
5. **Social proof grows over time:** "Join 100,000s of parents…" became "Join more than a million parents…".

**What their ads show** (Meta ad library via Kovert: 1,604 ads in total, 474 running today)
- Their **longest-running ads** (some over 400 days) are plain square images listing the product range. Each product gets one short benefit line and an emoji: "Bedtime Essentials: Support restful sleep without melatonin 😴". Plain, believable ads keep winning.
- Hooks talk straight to parents: "Hey parents — concerned about your kids' sleep quality?" followed by ✅/❌ lists.
- Offer: "Try it now for **50% off your first order**."
- Volume: hundreds of variations running at once. They test creative constantly, not one big campaign.

**Naming lesson:** "Hiya" is a greeting. It's friendly, short, and has nothing to do with age or vitamins. Each product name is just its benefit (*Kids Daily Greens & Superfoods*, *Daily Iron+*). The brand name carries the warmth, and the product names do the explaining.

## 4. Competitor analysis: OLLY

**Snapshot:** Founded in 2014–15 by Eric Ryan (co-founder of Method) and Brad Harrington. Gummies, sold first in mass retail (Target, Amazon, Sephora). Unilever bought it in 2019 and it is now a public benefit company. It has expanded to Singapore, China and Korea.

**What drives their growth**
1. **They sell outcomes, not ingredients.** Ryan: "Instead of selling biotin, we sell beauty. Instead of selling melatonin, we sell sleep." Product names are benefits or moods (*Goodbye Stress*, *Laser Focus*, *Undeniable Beauty*).
2. **The name was chosen to sound neither clinical nor folksy.** It's short, upbeat, has no fixed meaning, and the "O" has a smiley face. A name like that can stretch to any product.
3. **Design does the selling.** Bright colors and minimal packaging that "makes the supplement aisle feel less like a pharmacy." That's the Method approach.
4. **Millennial digital media:** long-form social video, influencers, and links straight to online stores.
5. **One master brand covers every age:** *Kids Gummy Worms*, *Teen Girl Multi*, adult lines, and *Brainy L'Olly Pops* for "kids 4+ and teens". This is the clearest evidence that one name can span kids, teens and adults when products carry the age.

**What this means for us**
- OLLY made its first kids' focus product a **lollipop with cartoon "Buddies" names**. That's playful but young. We can own the **credible, parent-trusted** space they leave open.
- OLLY markets **a branded ingredient (Cognizin)** the same way we would market Bacozest. Show the Bacozest name on the pack as proof ("Made with Bacozest®, studied in kids").

## 5. Molly Pittman (Smart Marketer): how her playbook applies

*Note: her name is spelled **Pittman**.*

**Who she is:** CEO and Managing Partner of Smart Marketer, which she co-founded with Ezra Firestone. Smart Marketer teaches ecommerce marketing, runs an agency, and operates its own DTC brands. She was previously VP of Marketing at DigitalMarketer, wrote *Click Happy* (2020), and co-hosts *The Smart Marketer Podcast*. She is best known for paid social and for treating marketing as a whole business function, not just ads.

**Her main ideas, and what to do with each here**

| Pittman principle | What it means | Kid's Focus application |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer avatar** | Define who you're talking to and *why*, including "the conversation in their head". | The three parent profiles are your avatars. Write each one's inner voice, e.g., the frustrated parent: *"I'm at my wits' end with homework every night."* |
| **Traffic temperature** | Cold, warm and hot audiences each need different ads and offers. Treat media buying like building a relationship. | **Cold:** situation hooks (homework battles, big feelings) leading to a plain-language study page. **Warm** (EC and BrainBoost buyers, site visitors): trial offer and the pediatrician one-pager. **Hot:** subscribe-and-save and the taste guarantee. |
| **Customer Value Journey** | Move people step by step: aware, engage, subscribe, convert, excite, ascend, advocate, promote. | It lines up with the plan's Notice → Trust → Try → Stay → Tell. Add the **Ascend** step: a second flavor, a family bundle, and later the teen product. |
| **Benefit-led hooks** | Her ~$600M ad-spend study found 88% of top ads had a clear benefit hook. Build creative from customer insight, not templates. | Lead with the result parents feel ("calmer homework time"), then the proof (the study). Use real parent quotes from the beta. |
| **Self-liquidating front-end offers** | An entry offer that covers its own ad cost, so the profit comes from lifetime value. | Test a 7-day "taste & try" pack at a low price, or "first box 50% off with subscription" (Hiya's offer), against the full price. |
| **A varied set of offers** | Different front-end offers for different segments, with more promotions for warm audiences. | Back-to-school bundle for the achievement-minded parent, risk-free trial for the worried researcher, "family calm kit" for the frustrated parent. |
| **Pick 2–3 strategies and go all in** | Focus beats spreading thin. | Year 1 core: Meta/YouTube (cold), email and SMS to EC customers (warm), Amazon reviews. Put social commerce and practitioners after that. |
| **Local brand building and community** (2025–26 episodes) | Organic, in-person community builds trust that paid ads can't buy. | Directly supports the Upstate SC plan: athletic fields, schools, parent ambassadors. |

**Practical use for the naming decision:** Pittman's method is to let the market vote. Run the three shortlisted names as identical cold Meta ads where only the name and pack mockup change, and compare click-through and landing-page sign-ups (section 6).

## 6. Naming exploration

> **Updated brief (Oct 9):** the brand will sell **more than brain supplements**. Focus & Calm is the **hero product**, and later products could include sleep, immunity, gut health, hydration, greens and multivitamins, first for ages 6–12 and later for teens. **The master brand has to fit every category, not just focus.**

### Naming criteria
1. **Fits every category:** it should sound just as right on a sleep powder or an immunity chew as on the focus product. *(new, and now the most important)*
2. **Grows with the child:** works at 6, at 14, and possibly for adults.
3. **Positive, trustworthy, warm, and grounded in evidence.**
4. **Safe for compliance:** no hint of disease or medication.
5. **Kids like it,** and it works with stickers, a reward chart and a mascot.
6. **We can own it:** trademark, domain, social handles, and Amazon search.

### What changed
- **Wits and Keel move from master brand to product name.** Both say "focus and calm", which made them strong for one product, but *Wits Immune* or *Keel Greens* don't make sense. Wits is still an excellent **name for the hero product** (see below).
- **The Scouts idea gets stronger.** Exploring, growing and "gear for the adventure of growing up" fit any category, so a scouting-style brand can cover focus, sleep, immunity and hydration. The trademark, endorsement and teen problems are unchanged, so the **theme** is worth keeping even if the word "Scouts" isn't.
- **Hiya and OLLY show the model:** each has a **meaning-light, warm master brand** and products named for their benefit. Neither name says what the products do, which is why both could keep adding categories.

### Brand structure

```
MASTER BRAND (fits every category, warm, grows with the child)
 ├── Hero:  [Brand] Wits — Focus & Calm Daily Stick   (Bacozest®, studied in kids)
 ├── Next:  [Brand] Sleep · [Brand] Immune · [Brand] Gut · [Brand] Hydrate · [Brand] Greens
 └── Later: [Brand] Teen line (Teen Focus / Study Days, Teen Sleep, Teen Sport)
```

Name the hero product (e.g., **Wits**) so it can build its own reputation, the way OLLY's *Goodbye Stress* or Hiya's *Bedtime Essentials* did. Later products can just use a plain descriptor.

### Plays on "happy"

**Why "happy" is the right instinct:** happy is the result parents actually want, and it fits every category. The team called this "an anti-frustration product". A focused kid means happy homework time, good sleep means happy mornings, and good immunity means happy school days. A happy-themed name says why the brand exists without naming any one category or age.

**The catch:** the word **"Happy" alone is crowded** in kids' food and supplements. Happy Family Organics (*Happy Baby*, *Happy Tot*, *Happy Kid*) is the big one, and Class 5 trademarks include *HEALTHY KIDS HAPPY KIDS*, *HAPPY FAMILIES* and *Happi Kidz* (an Indian kids' gummy brand). A **clever twist** on happy is both more distinctive and easier to own than "Happy ___".

**Making it feel intentional, not just cute:** the name, the promise and the box should all tell one story.
- **Promise:** "Happier days, together." This maps to the plan's fourth pillar, *Better days together*.

### Why Wayfinder is the lead master-brand recommendation
- **It keeps the concept you like:** the explorer, scouting spirit, without borrowing the Scouting organizations' word.
- **It fits every category:** "finding your way" works for focus (homework), sleep (bedtime), immunity (the school year) and sport (game day). Each product is *gear for the journey*.
- **It grows with the child:** it's adventurous for a 7-year-old, aspirational for a 15-year-old, and credible for adults. Teens are literally finding their way.
- **It builds trust:** a wayfinder is a guide. That fits the "proof, not promises" voice and the pediatrician one-pager.
- **It works for kids:** a trail map reward chart, "badges" for each product, and a compass or North Star mascot. Most of the Scouts ideas carry over.
- *Watch-outs:* three syllables is longer than Hiya or OLLY, so test it out loud with kids. It also needs a full trademark check, because "wayfinder" is a common word in software and travel, though we found nothing in supplements.

### If the team still wants "Scout"
Use the **singular** (*Scout Wits Focus & Calm*, *Scout Sleep*), run legal clearance **first**, and keep all creative clear of uniforms, badges that look like scouting insignia, and troop language, so nobody reads it as a Scouting endorsement. A middle path: use **Wayfinder** as the master brand and **Scout as the mascot** (a curious fox or owl who earns badges with the kids).

### Taglines (fit every category)
- "Gear for growing up."
- "Proof, not promises."
- "Better days, together."
- For the hero product: "Focus and calm, studied in kids." / "For families at their wits' end."

## 7. Recommended next steps (fits the October Foundation phase)

1. **This week:** The team cuts the list to three master-brand names (suggested: **Wayfinder, Scout, Field Day**) and agrees on **Wits** as the hero product name. A legal knockout search runs on all four at the same time.
2. **Weeks 2–3: market test (Pittman method).** Run identical cold Meta ads where only the name, logo and pack mockup change. Use $300–500 per name, aimed at parents of 6–12 year olds. Measure click-through, cost per landing-page sign-up, and a one-question poll ("Which would you trust for your child?"). In parallel, show five or six kids from employee families the packs and ask which one they'd want.
3. **Week 4:** Pick the name. File the trademark (needed for Amazon Brand Registry), lock the domain and social handles, and brief the designer on the master brand plus descriptor system.
4. **Update Phase 2** with OLLY Focus Buddies and Mellow Buddies, and keep an eye on Hiya (a likely future entrant into focus, with a million-parent customer base).
